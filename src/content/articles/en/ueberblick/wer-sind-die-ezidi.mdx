## Self-designation and the name of the religion

The community calls itself <Begriff id="ezidi" />; the religion is <Begriff id="ezidiyati" /> in Kurmancî, usually “Ezidentum” in German and
“Yezidism” in English. There is no single standard spelling used
worldwide.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 32" />

### Where the name comes from

This is unresolved. Several derivations compete in the literature, among them
Middle Persian *yaz(a)d* (“god”) and Kurdish *Ezda(yî)* in the sense of
“creator”.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 33" /> A further thesis traces the name
to the Umayyad caliph Yazid I; it is contested, and the page on names sets out
the counter-arguments.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 33–34" />

### Spellings

Alongside “Êzîdî”, English texts commonly use “Yezidis” and
“Yazidis”.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 32" /> The 2016 report of the UN
Commission of Inquiry uses “Yazidis”
throughout.<Cite id="un-coi-2016-they-came-to-destroy" loc="Titel" /> In German,
“Eziden”, “Jesiden” and “Yeziden” all occur; according to Tagay and Ortaç, Êzîdî
living in Germany now mostly use “Eziden”.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 32" />
Since its 2023 resolution the German Bundestag has written “Êzîdinnen und
Êzîden”, explaining that it follows the community's own
name.<Cite id="bundestag-2023-anerkennung" loc="S. 1, Fußnote" />

<Randglosse titel="Why this portal writes Êzîdî" art="sprache">

  a judgement on the other spellings – it makes the spelling predictable across

</Randglosse>

## Where Êzîdî live

### Regions of origin

The traditional settlement areas lie in northern Iraq, north-eastern Syria,
south-eastern Turkey and western Iran – within the area inhabited by
Kurds.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 29" /> The main settlement area is the
northern Iraqi province of Nineveh, above all the districts of <Begriff id="singal" /> and <Begriff id="sexan" />; according to a 2012 figure
from the US State Department, between 600,000 and 700,000 people of Êzîdî faith
lived there.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 30" />

These figures describe the situation before 3 August 2014. Since then the
distribution has changed fundamentally: after the attack by the so-called
Islamic State, the UN Commission of Inquiry found that no free Êzîdî remained in
the Şingal region; the community of 400,000 living there had been wholly
displaced, abducted or
killed.<Cite id="un-coi-2016-they-came-to-destroy" loc="Abs. 175" />

### Diaspora

The largest diaspora community lives in Germany. Sources differ on its size –
Tagay and Ortaç give roughly 100,000 people for
2016,<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 30" /> while the Federal Agency for
Civic Education gives “up to 250,000” in 2024.<Cite id="bpb-2024-voelkermord" />
The main states are Lower Saxony, North Rhine-Westphalia, Hesse and
Berlin.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 30" /> Further communities exist in
Armenia, Georgia and Russia – together some 100,000
people.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 30" />

## Religion in outline

What follows says only as much as can be said without going deeper. The section
on religion carries the account further.

### Concept of God

Êzîdîtism is a monotheistic religion: Êzîdî believe in one God, <Begriff id="xwede" />, creator of the universe.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 41" />
According to the Brandenburg handout, belief in this one God is central to the
religion; personifying evil as an independent power is expressly rejected,
because a second power alongside God would call God's omnipotence into
question.<Cite id="raa-brandenburg-2018" loc="S. 3" />

In the Êzîdî cosmogony God first created the world in the form of a white,
flawless pearl; from his light he created seven
angels.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 41" />

### Tawûsî Melek

A central role belongs to <Begriff id="tawusi-melek" />, literally “Peacock
Angel”.<Cite id="raa-brandenburg-2018" loc="S. 3" /> Kreyenbroek and Omarkhali
describe him as “Lord of this World”, responsible for everything that happens on
earth – from a human point of view for good and for
ill.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 123" />

The notion that Êzîdî venerate the principle of evil rests on a misreading of
exactly this role.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 123" />

### Oral tradition

Religious tradition was passed on orally for centuries. It is told in several
forms: alluded to in the poetic sacred texts, the <Begriff id="qewl" />,
expounded in sermons (*mishabet*) or narrated in prose
(*çîrok*).<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 125" /> Two
short texts long taken to be “sacred books” of the religion were later shown to
be spurious.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 122" />

<Randglosse titel="Practice before doctrine" art="forschung">
  Few studies treat Êzîdî articles of belief – according to Kreyenbroek and
  Omarkhali a consequence of the fact that right practice, not right doctrine,
  stands at the centre.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 126" />
  Anyone expecting a list of dogmas is looking in the wrong place.
</Randglosse>

## Social order in outline

### The three groups

The community is divided into three endogamous groups: the two “priestly”
groups, <Begriff id="sex" /> and <Begriff id="pir" />, and the <Begriff id="mirid" />, the
laity.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 124" /> Kreyenbroek
and Omarkhali describe the social structure as a web of mutual bonds and
obligations; filling one's role within it was traditionally regarded as an
essential part of being
Êzîdî.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 124" />

The Brandenburg handout stresses that the responsibility of these groups extends
solely to religious matters and expressly not to worldly hierarchy; they must
therefore “by no means be confused with the caste system in
Hinduism”.<Cite id="raa-brandenburg-2018" loc="S. 5" />

<Randglosse titel="A note on wording" art="sprache">
  English-language scholarship uses the word “castes” for these groups – in
  Kreyenbroek and Omarkhali, however, in quotation
  marks.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 124" /> This page
  says “groups” and avoids any wording that asserts a rank order.
</Randglosse>

### Rules of marriage

Membership passes through descent. The UN Commission of Inquiry records that a
child must have two Êzîdî parents and that conversion to the religion is
theologically impossible.<Cite id="un-coi-2016-they-came-to-destroy" loc="Abs. 19" />
Alongside the requirement to marry within the community, Kreyenbroek and
Omarkhali name the honour concept *namûs* as a second formative norm; it places
great weight on the reserved conduct of the women of a
family.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 124" />

After the attacks by the so-called Islamic State, the religious leadership
declared that the abducted women remain full members of the
community.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 126" />

## Language

The community's shared language is <Begriff id="kurmanci" />.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 122" />
Tagay and Ortaç name one exception: in Behzanê and Baʻşîqe, Arabic is
predominantly spoken.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 29" /> In Armenia, <Begriff id="ezdiki" /> is recognised by the state as a language name in its own
right.<Cite id="raa-brandenburg-2018" loc="S. 3" />

## History of persecution in outline

### The firman tradition

In oral tradition the number 72 plays an essential part; alongside “72 peoples”,
there is talk of 72 <Begriff id="firman" />.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 174" />
The term properly denotes ordinances and decrees of Islamic rulers; Êzîdî use it
for the massacres and pogroms they were subjected to over the
centuries.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 174" /> The number is meant
symbolically; the events behind it, according to Tagay and Ortaç, are not – they
rest on historical circumstances.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 174" />

As a reason for their particular exposure in the Ottoman Empire, the authors note
that Êzîdî were not regarded there as belonging to a religion of the book and so
could not, unlike Christian and Jewish communities, rely on state protection in
return for the corresponding tax
payments.<Cite id="tagay-ortac-2016" loc="S. 174" />

The UN Commission of Inquiry draws the same connection: false ideas about the
Êzîdî faith underlay cycles of persecution reaching back at least to Ottoman
times.<Cite id="un-coi-2016-they-came-to-destroy" loc="Abs. 19" />

### The genocide from 2014

On the morning of 3 August 2014 the so-called Islamic State attacked the
settlement areas in Şingal.<Cite id="bpb-2024-voelkermord" /> In 2016 the UN
Commission of Inquiry concluded that the group had committed genocide against the
Êzîdî, along with multiple crimes against humanity and war
crimes.<Cite id="un-coi-2016-they-came-to-destroy" loc="Zusammenfassung" /> The
German Bundestag recognised the crimes as genocide in January 2023, adopting the
legal assessment of the UN investigative team
UNITAD.<Cite id="bundestag-2023-anerkennung" loc="S. 1" />

On orders of magnitude the sources give: more than 5,000 people killed and about
7,000 abducted; by 2016, according to the UN Human Rights Council, 3,500 girls
and women had been freed or ransomed, while around 2,700 remained missing
according to Pro Asyl.<Cite id="bpb-2024-voelkermord" />

## Common misconceptions

**“Devil-worshippers”.** The UN Commission of Inquiry expressly calls the
widespread view of the Êzîdî faith as a religion of “devil-worshippers” “wholly
incorrect”.<Cite id="un-coi-2016-they-came-to-destroy" loc="Abs. 19" /> It goes
back to a misreading of the role of Tawûsî
Melek.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 123" /> In the
German-speaking world Karl May entrenched this image with *Durchs wilde
Kurdistan* (1892), portraying Êzîdî as noble, well-meaning
“devil-worshippers”.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 122–123" />

**“An offshoot of Islam”.** This reading goes back to M. Guidi and was widely
accepted in scholarship around
1930.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 123" /> More recent
scholarship instead takes up the Êzîdî view of their religion as a tradition in
its own right.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 123" />

**“There are sacred books”.** The two texts held to be such have been shown to be
spurious.<Cite id="kreyenbroek-omarkhali-2016-einfuehrung" loc="S. 122" />
